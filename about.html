About the Project

ReStorying Campus Space is a collaborative archival research project that critically examines the situated history of the University of California, San Diego campus. The project treats the built environment of the university – architectural choices, land designations, spatial arrangements – as archival objects that carry histories that seldom appear in official institutional narratives. Using archival photographs and administrative records, ReStorying Campus Space explores the UCSD campus as a site of inquiry, by asking questions like: What might we learn through a collective reading, walking, roaming, pointing, looking, and/or attending to UCSD’s campus and the land it occupies? What histories do we inherit in being a part of this institution, and what can we do with these histories? And ultimately, how might we then (re)position ourselves in relation to this institution?

Twenty student researchers were trained through the project to develop skills in reading institutional records against their administrative purposes and to translate archival findings into publicly-accessible media documentations. This project reframes the university campus as a mediated site of struggle by giving students authorship over how their campus's history is told.

This project was made possible through support from the Summer Graduate Teaching Scholarship through the UCSD Teaching and Learning Commons and course development support from the UCSD Department of Communication. For more information, contact Vandhana Ravi at vravi@ucsd.edu.

Methods

(1)Doing the Reading

As part of training for student research work, we spent the first half of the course period reading theoretical texts in critical university studies together. As an instructor, I prioritize modeling active reading together as a way for students to learn to work with complex ideas and to think critically about how research and theory are developed. Some strategies we used in this course included (1) Gallery Walks where students moved physically between excerpts of the chapter, annotating and responding to each passage in shared space before the group reconvened to discuss what had accumulated, (2) Page Partners where students circulated the space to find the classmate holding the same page, then read and discuss that shared passage together before reconvening as a full group, (3) Annotation Relay, where a shared copy of the text circulates, and each student adds one annotation before passing it on, and (4) Reading Aloud, where students read a short passage of a text aloud, often word by word, moving around the room, which slows the pace of a dense theoretical passage and makes its syntax audible. Collective reading is a way of practicing the kind of critical attention to text and ideas about space that they can carry into their own research.

(2)Re-Viewing our Campus

A central priority of this course was to go physically outside the classroom, replacing seminar discussion with a series of site-based encounters across the university grounds. We did this over a few months through a number of collaborations. The first was an alternative campus tour led by UCSD students through student-led, community, and activist spaces on campus that the university's official tours usually route around. We reflected on what we learned from this tour by creating site-based archival poetry that asked students to compose collage poems from UCSD Dis-orientation guides from the 1970s to the present day. Students were asked to place their poems across the university campus, treating the campus itself as a text to be read against through student poetry. You can learn more about this work and read the student poems here. In addition to collage poems, students reoriented their thinking about the skies and lands that the campus illegally inhabits through engagements with Kumeyaay Cosmology and created ink-based art depicting Kumeyaay constellations, each imparting its own fable and mythology through a class visit to the Museum of History and Culture La Jolla for an exhibit curated by the Kumeyaay Visual Storytelling Project.

(3) Checking the Record

Student researchers investigated their campus sites primarily through archival research of UCSD’s institutional records. To teach archival methods, the course assignments followed a sequence of discrete, cumulative steps rather than a single research assignment delivered all at once. Students began by working directly with archival images, learning to read a historical photograph as a cultural text. From there, the course built toward an annotated bibliography, asking students to track which sources were available and how each source was produced and made accessible. Each stage layered onto the last, so that by the time students moved into their own individual site-based research, they had already practiced methods like close reading, source evaluation, and citation as a form of accountability. These assignments were co-designed with Heather Smedberg and Alanna Aiko Moore from UCSD’s Geisel Library and Special Collections to ensure librarians with expertise in the collections' holdings taught student researchers how to use library resources and to use best practices that are mindful of archival labor and all workers who use their bodyminds to make these archives accessible to students.
